# Build brief: band 2.7, say it to a planner

Silurian Assay. Remediation between sprint 2 and sprint 3.

Source: the first test with a supply chain planner who did not build the tool and was told nothing about it, run 2 September. `planner-test-findings.md` has the full record. Read it before this brief.

Sprint 2 passed on substance. The volume ranked open items list sent the planner to the right line, they reproduced three of the five discontinued resolution codes unprompted, and they refused to forecast a line whose status was unconfirmed. **The mechanism works. The words do not.**

---

## 1. The diagnosis

The tool speaks its own vocabulary fluently and never teaches it. The planner's seven confusions and the product owner's four points are one finding, not eleven.

That produces a specific commercial risk. A planner who cannot read the screen alone needs a consultant beside them, and at that point the finding lives in the consultant rather than in the product. What gets sold is time, not software.

---

## 2. Two rules that govern everything in this band

**Plain, not friendly.** Do not soften the register. The site's voice is short, declarative and unhedged, and that is what makes it credible. "Great news, your data looks mostly fine" is worse than what we have now, not better. The enemy is unexplained vocabulary, not directness. `caveated` is not too blunt, it is unexplained. `ADI 3.89` is not too cold, it is untranslated.

**Explanation on demand, never in the reading path.** The planner already said the routing summary was dense enough to skip. Answering "explain more" by adding text to the flow makes that worse. Every explanation added in this band must be available when reached for and invisible when not: a term that explains itself when touched, a panel line of one sentence, a glossary one click away. If the default screen gets longer, the change is wrong.

---

## 3. The stories

### 2.7.1 Reconcile accept against not usable, and state readiness plainly

The planner could not tell whether the run had been accepted, rejected or partly accepted. Validation accepted the file; quality banded the portfolio not usable. Both are correct and nothing on screen relates them.

Replace the two bare verdicts with one sentence of the shape:

> Your file was accepted and processed. 7 of 14 lines can be forecast. 5 need an answer from you first, covering 25.39 percent of volume. 2 need a commercial decision rather than a forecast.

**Correction, recorded 3 September.** Rev 1.0 of this brief said "Nine of fourteen". That was wrong and the build session caught it. Nine is fourteen minus the five open items, which silently counts the two `policy_only` lines as forecastable. They are not eligible and they carry no refusal. `expected_routing.json` v1.1 routes seven lines eligible. The sentence needs the third clause so all fourteen lines are accounted for, because a headline that does not add up is defect 1 in a new form, and counting policy-only lines as forecastable is the exact conflation story 2.7.4 exists to expose. The brief reproduced the defect it was written to fix.

The stage verdicts stay available underneath for anyone who wants them. The readable sentence comes first.

**Acceptance:** a reader can say, in one attempt, whether the run succeeded and how much of their portfolio is ready, and the line counts in the sentence sum to the portfolio.

### 2.7.2 Rewrite the two action texts that failed

These are the two the product sells, and both failed. Substance below; write the wording, keep the register.

**`model_eligible_wide_interval`.** The planner understood the uncertainty and not what to do differently. It must name the action:

> Demand arrives every month, but the size swings widely. Forecast the range rather than the number. Size the buffer from the spread and the service level you have promised, and expect the monthly figure to be wrong in both directions. Chasing the average here adds work and no accuracy.

**`policy_only`.** The planner inferred the meaning and could not see the next action. It must offer choices and a first step:

> Orders arrive rarely and vary widely in size. No forecasting method will predict this line, so the answer is an arrangement rather than a number. Pick one: agree committed volumes with the customer, make to order against an agreed lead time, or hold a buffer you have priced and accepted. Start by asking the customer how they actually order.

**Acceptance:** a planner reading either line can state a next action without asking a question. Test it on the second planner, not on us.

### 2.7.3 Make the detail drawer discoverable

The planner asked for a way to open a SKU and see its history, findings and options together. That drawer has existed since story 2.1. They did not find it.

Do not redesign the drawer. Make the row invite the click: an affordance on the row, a cursor state, a hint on first use that dismisses itself. Cheapest possible change that gets it opened.

**Acceptance:** a first-time user opens a detail drawer within a minute without being told it exists.

### 2.7.4 Show why a line is on the open items list

Five lines were open and other ineligible lines were not. The refusal against not eligible distinction is invisible, and the specification session got it wrong too, which is the evidence that it needs saying rather than assuming.

State it where the list is:

> These lines are waiting on an answer from you. Lines that need a commercial decision rather than a data answer are not listed here.

**Acceptance:** a reader can explain why a policy-only line is absent from the list.

### 2.7.5 One glossary, one source

Every term and acronym defined once, in plain language, in one file. Rendered in the tool and in the report's method appendix from that same source. Two copies of a definition drift, which is the same rule as one implementation of any judgement.

Cover at least: the three quality bands, the five demand states, the seven routing decisions, the resolution codes, ADI, CV squared, ABC volume class, XYZ, manifest, bundle, run, pass.

Definitions are written for a planner, not a statistician:

- **ADI.** How often demand arrives. 1.0 means every period. 3.0 means roughly one period in three.
- **CV squared.** How much order sizes vary when demand does arrive. Low means steady sizes. High means some orders are far larger than others.
- **Caveated.** Usable, with something you should know. The finding is named on the line, and it is your judgement whether it changes what you do.
- **Not usable.** This line's history cannot support a forecast in this run. Something has to change in the data before a number would mean anything.

That last pair answers the planner's question about the operational difference between the two bands, which is the point.

**Acceptance:** every specialist term on the screen appears in the glossary, proven by a test that walks the rendered copy and fails on an undefined term. The report appendix and the tool render identical text from one file.

### 2.7.6 Panel purpose lines and terms that explain themselves

Every panel opens with **one sentence** saying what it is for, in the planner's language. Not a paragraph.

Every specialist term explains itself where it appears, drawn from the glossary. Hover, tap, expand, your call, but it must not add height to the default view.

**Acceptance:** the default screen is no longer than it is today. Measure it and say so in the pull request.

### 2.7.7 Say what the tool does before a file is uploaded

Today the landing state is a file box. A stranger should understand what they are about to get before they hand over data.

Three or four lines, plain, no marketing: what it checks, what it will tell them, what it will refuse to do, and what it does with their file. The last of those is not decoration. A client's first question about any upload is where the data goes, and the answer already exists in the data handling position from story 1.5.

**Acceptance:** someone who has never seen the tool can say what it will do for them before uploading anything.

### 2.7.8 Demote provenance and compress the routing summary

The name hash and run identifier "did not help me make a planning decision." They serve a consultant defending a run, not a planner working one. Move them behind a control rather than removing them: reproducibility is a selling point to the client's IT function and a liability on a planner's screen.

The routing summary paragraph was dense enough that the planner said they would skip it. Cut it to the sentence from 2.7.1 plus the split by reason.

**Acceptance:** nothing is deleted, only relocated, and the run is still fully reproducible from what remains reachable.

## 4. How this band is accepted

**Not by us agreeing it reads better.** By a second planner, who has not seen the tool, run through the same protocol in `planner-test-pack.md`.

The bar: they can state what the run concluded, what is waiting on them, and what to do about one refused line and one policy-only line, without asking a question.

That is the same test that just found these eleven items. If it finds a twelfth, the band is not finished.

---

## 5. Out of scope

- Visual design and the branded export. Story 5.1.
- Inventory, stock and supply figures. Sprint 6, and the planner is right that policy-only lines cannot be actioned without them.
- Money and value at risk. Sprint 4.
- SKU context fields, owners and due dates, run-to-run comparison. Backlog, sprints 4 and 5.
- Any change to a decision, a threshold or an engine. This band changes words and where they sit, nothing else. If a test that asserts behaviour has to change, stop and raise it.
